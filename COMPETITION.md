# Metty — competitive analysis

Researched 3 September 2026. Prices are as observed on that date; anything unverified is
marked. This document is deliberately written as the case *against* as much as the case
for — a competitive analysis that only finds strengths is marketing.

## The five findings that matter

1. **The closest competitor died eight months ago.** Now & Zen, Inc. — maker of the Zen
   Timepiece, the only well-known consumer product that mechanically strikes a real brass
   bowl — ceased trading on **17 January 2026**, on its 31st anniversary. No repairs, no
   parts, no successor.
2. **The core feature is already free, and better specified than expected.** Insight
   Timer's free tier does *exactly* Metty's bell pattern — three strikes to open, one at
   each boundary, three to close — as a named, saved preset. Metty has **no functional
   advantage** on its headline feature.
3. **But nobody times a *practice*.** Every physical competitor times a *duration*.
   Nothing on the market has a programme model with stages, repeats, and a bell that
   means "change the object of attention."
4. **The £150–250 retail band is proven fatal.** It is exactly where the Zen Timepiece
   sat, and it was too expensive to be an impulse buy and too cheap to fund a real
   object with a real bowl. The live band for a wooden box that strikes a real bowl is
   **€550–900**, held by a single German maker.
5. **A €129 mechanical rival launches this quarter.** minagi is aesthetically adjacent,
   fully mechanical, and will make the sub-£150 end noisy from Q4 2026.

## What Metty is actually competing with

Not other meditation timers. The honest competitive set, in order of how many people use
it:

1. **A phone with a free app on it.** The incumbent by an enormous margin; everything
   below is a rounding error against it.
2. **A phone with the stock clock app on it.** More common than the wellness industry
   likes to admit.
3. **Nothing.** Sitting until it feels like enough.
4. **A real bowl and a person to strike it.** The traditional answer, still the best one,
   and unavailable when you sit alone.
5. Only then: other objects you could buy.

Any analysis that starts at (5) is flattering itself. The question is not "is Metty
better than the other meditation timers" but **"what does a physical object do that a
free app on a device you already own does not"** — and the answer has to survive contact
with someone who is not already sold.

### The one structural argument

There is exactly one argument that holds up, and everything else in the pitch is
downstream of it:

**Using a meditation app requires picking up the phone.** The object you are trying to
get away from for forty minutes is the object you must handle to begin, and the object
sitting within arm's reach throughout. Even in aeroplane mode, even face-down, it is a
device whose entire design intent is to fragment attention — and it is now part of the
ritual of starting.

A single-purpose object removes the phone from the practice completely. That is not an
aesthetic preference; it is a functional difference in what the practice consists of.
The real bell, the wooden case, the Braun idiom, the absence of a subscription are all
*supporting evidence for the same claim*: this is a thing you use instead of a screen,
not another screen.

## The direct field: devices that strike a real bowl

Nearly empty. One German maker trading, one unlaunched Kickstarter, one dead American
company, one uncommercialised design project.

### Now & Zen Zen Timepiece — the cautionary tale

6″ brass bowl-gong on a hardwood base, 2× C cells, solenoid plunger, alarm + countdown +
interval + hourly mindfulness bell. Two knobs set "Lo" and "Hi" strike power, and the
manual talks about hunting for the sweet spot.

**Why it matters is how it failed.** Thirty-one years, coverage in the NYT and on Good
Morning America, and it still ended. The failure modes are legible in the record and
every one of them is a design lesson Metty can bank:

- **The bowl was not fixed.** It rested near the plunger, so any nudge silenced it.
  Reviewers' single biggest complaint. Metty's cradle work directly answers this.
- **An escalating alarm** people found aggressive — one reviewer described it as "a monk
  losing his temper."
- **A 40-page manual** for a device with two knobs and a mode button. It lost on control
  legibility, not on sound.
- **A New Age aesthetic that dated badly** — Wikipedia records reviewers calling it a
  "hippie carpenter contraption."
- **$189 couldn't fund better engineering.**

Metty wins on every axis where that product was weak. It inherits none of the goodwill.

### Dayasa — the honest competitor

Handmade Tibetan, Nepalese, Indian or Japanese bowl on a solid German wood body, struck
by a clapper with **10 adjustable strength levels**. Alarm, hourly gong, countdown,
interval function. Battery-powered, and explicitly marketed on avoiding electromagnetic
radiation. **€549–899** depending on wood and which bowl you choose.

This is the product that proves the price. Its weaknesses are precisely Metty's
openings: it is fundamentally *an alarm clock* with a meditation mode bolted on, it is
driven by a **touch display** (the opposite of tactile dials), and it has no programme
concept, no stage or repeat model, and no doctrinal grounding.

⚠️ One source put Dayasa "from €489" and another at €549–899. Treat the band as
approximate.

### minagi — the dangerous one

Launching Q4 2026 at **€99 early bird / €129 retail**, 500 numbered pieces. Fully
mechanical spring-driven clockwork, solid walnut 10 cm cube, solid brass bowl tuned
400–500 Hz, soft-hammer striker. No screen, no app, no battery. You turn a brass knob to
one of six presets (5/10/15/30/45/60 min) and release; the dial position shows time
remaining; the hammer strikes at zero. Their language — "take back your attention", "full
attention. No distraction." — is the language Metty would use.

**It is dangerous because it looks similar in a photograph at a quarter of the price.**
It is also a *focus/Pomodoro* object, not a meditation instrument: one bell at the end,
no opening triad, no stage boundaries, no repeats, six fixed durations. It cannot express
a Mettā Bhāvanā. Metty's answer is that it times the practice, not the duration — but
Metty must never be priced as though it competes with this.

### Chime — independent arrival at the same mechanism

Nour Malaeb's 2016 project: Arduino, solenoid striker, LED ring, and **the bowl itself as
a capacitive touch sensor** — rest your hand on it to start. Never commercialised.
Useful validation that the mechanism is right, and the capacitive-bowl input is a more
elegant interaction than any dial. Worth knowing about; not a threat.

## The empty category: institutional gear

A real finding, and worth sitting with. **Monastic and retreat practice does not use
automated bells.**

- **Plum Village** sells hand bells only — €56 (7 cm) and €95 (9 cm), with cushion and
  inviter. Their automation answer is a **phone app**, which is the exact thing Metty is
  positioned against.
- **DharmaCrafts**, the main US Buddhist retail channel, lists four items under "Timers,
  Bells and Gongs" and **all four are sold out**. A Buddhist retailer with essentially one
  electronic timer in range, unavailable.
- **Automated gong strikers exist only as industrial school and factory bells** — Indian
  manufacturers selling 9″ and 12″ electromagnetic gongs on programmable timers. Loud,
  ugly, functional.

Nobody has brought that mechanism into a considered domestic object except Now & Zen,
Dayasa and now minagi. But note the other reading: the human invitation is part of the
practice, and retreat centres ring by hand **by choice**, not for want of a machine.
Worth deciding whether Metty leans into or away from that.

## The volume competitor: speaker bowls

Loud, cheap and growing. These use samples, not struck metal.

- **ZenBowl** (Oaiosys) raised **$88,098 from 894 backers** on Kickstarter — 652% of goal,
  "Project We Love" — for a sampled 8-frequency bowl. ⚠️ Kickstarter hosts a DMCA notice
  against the project and there is now an Indiegogo "coming soon" page; fulfilment could
  not be verified.
- Amazon is saturated with "automatic Tibetan singing bowl machines" at **$25–70** —
  7–38 sampled tones, seven-colour night lights, USB-C.

**Strategic read:** ~900 people will pay for a "singing bowl device" sight-unseen. The
low end is being commoditised by sampled audio with LED lighting. Metty's "no speaker, no
samples" claim is genuine and defensible — but the market has been trained to accept a
speaker, so it has to be **demonstrated in sound**, not asserted in copy.

## The real incumbent: apps

| App | Model | Multi-stage interval bells? |
|---|---|---|
| **Insight Timer** | Free core; MemberPlus $9.99/mo | **Yes, fully** — bells at arbitrary offsets, **1, 2 or 3 strikes each**, saved as named presets |
| **Ensō** | Timer + 1 bell free forever; bells $0.99 | **Yes** — intervals, prep timer, screen-off, chimes through silent mode |
| **Medito** | Free, nonprofit, open source | Intervals and silent gaps; simpler than Insight |
| **Plum Village** | Free, no ads, no IAP | Customisable timer + mindfulness bell |
| Calm / Headspace / Balance | $70–130/yr | Basic timer only |
| Waking Up | $99–130/yr, free on request | Timer; staged support ⚠️ unverified |

**This is the most uncomfortable finding in the document.** Insight Timer's *free* timer
already produces three strikes to open, one at each boundary and three to close, saved as
a preset. Ensō does the same more elegantly. On its headline feature, Metty is matched at
zero cost by software the buyer already has.

### And the tradition-specific case is covered too

- **Wildmind (wildmind.org)** gives away MP3 timers built explicitly for the five-stage
  Mettā Bhāvanā (20/30/40/60 min) and four-stage mindfulness of breathing, with "bells to
  mark the start, the transition to each stage, and the end." Free.
- **FreeBuddhistAudio** hosts "Bells for Meditation — 5 Stages (Metta Bhavana)". Free.
- **No official Triratna app was found** ⚠️ (absence of evidence, not proof). Some
  Triratna centres run online sits *through Insight Timer*.

Note also the practice reality: Triratna stages are conventionally **equal length**, so a
plain repeating interval bell is already sufficient. **Metty's programmes are
conveniences, not capabilities.** That is worth stating plainly, because the pitch is
tempted to claim otherwise.

## Prior art in DIY

| Project | Approach |
|---|---|
| **Chime** (Nour Malaeb, 2016) | Arduino MKR1000, solenoid, bowl as capacitive sensor, LED ring |
| **Adafruit Yoga Pose Chime** | CLUE board, proximity gesture, transistor-driven solenoid on a real bowl |
| **Motorized Meditation Timer** (Alex Mikes) | Arduino + **servo** trip-hammer, keypad and LCD |
| Arduino Forum #942128 | Long-running solenoid-vs-servo debate |

Recurring complaints across all of them: **actuator noise contaminating the strike**,
solenoid power supply, and mallet/strike-force tuning to get a musical rather than
percussive hit. All three are addressed in `hardware/README.md`, which is reassuring —
these are the known failure modes and the plans already take them seriously.

**Nobody in this set builds multi-stage timing.** They all do start and end. That is a
real, if narrow, gap.

language, not its price band, and should be careful not to inherit its anchor by
borrowing its vocabulary too closely.

## Screen-free positioning, and what subtraction sells for

| Product | Price | Note |
|---|---|---|
| **Light Phone III** | **$799**, next wave $899 | Launched ~$599–699 and **rose twice**, still selling |
| **Daylight DC-1** | $729–799 | "Calm tablet" |
| **Punkt MP02** | $299–379 | Jasper Morrison design — **refurbished-only on Punkt's own site** |
| **Yoto Player** | £89.99 | Screen-free kids' audio |
| **Analogue Pocket** | $240 | Raised from $220; "considered object" premium |

**The key lesson: subtraction is not a discount argument.** Light Phone raised its price
twice and kept selling. Punkt is the counterexample — beautiful, correctly positioned,
and reduced to selling refurbs. Punkt's problem was that it asked you to *give something
up*. Metty asks you to give up nothing you were using.

| $25–70 | Time Timer; Zenimal; Amazon sampled-bowl machines |
| £75–160 | Morphée £74.95; Yoto £89.99; Enso Clock $99; **minagi €129**; Plum Village bell €95; Moonbird £159 |

| $730–900 | Daylight DC-1; Light Phone III |
| £3,600 | Laikingland "Just About Now" — struck gong as art object |

**The inference is uncomfortable but clear.** There is a live, occupied band at roughly
**£480–780** for a wooden object that strikes a real bowl, held by Dayasa alone and
vacated at the US end by Now & Zen. Light Phone and Daylight independently confirm
£700–800 is survivable for a screen-refusing single-purpose object.

**A caution on the Rams argument:** the actual Braun object Metty quotes — the BC22 alarm
clock — sells for **$69**. Design language alone does not price at £500. Teenage
Engineering charges $579–1,499, but on fifteen years of brand equity. Metty's realistic
justification is the **bowl, the wood, the low-volume manufacture and the hand assembly**
— the Dayasa argument — not the Braun one.

## Where the gap genuinely is

Not "a timer", and not "a better bell". It is **a durable, repairable, non-networked
ritual object for people already committed to a staged practice**.

Three things have never been combined: a real struck bowl, a genuine programme model with
stages and repeats, and serious industrial design. Dayasa has the bowl and the wood but
no programmes. Salubrion has 50 intervals but a speaker. Now & Zen had the bowl and is
gone. minagi has the object but times a duration, not a practice.

The buyer is not choosing Metty over Insight Timer on features. **They are buying a
reason to leave the phone outside the room.**

## Where Metty is weak

**1. The core feature is free, twice over.** Insight Timer's free tier does the exact bell
pattern; Wildmind gives away the exact Triratna staged audio. Metty's functional claim is
already met at zero cost, on hardware the buyer owns.

**2. "Real bell, not a speaker" is a weaker argument than it feels.** A £20 Bluetooth
speaker and a free app reproduce a well-recorded bowl at a quality most sitters cannot
distinguish with eyes closed at three metres. The honest case is not fidelity — it is the
object's **presence in the room and its refusal to be anything else**. That is a ritual
argument, not an acoustic one. If the pitch leans on sound quality, a sceptic wins.

**3. The staging advantage is thinner than it looks.** Triratna stages are equal-length by
convention, so a repeating interval bell already covers the practice.

**4. One bowl, one tone, forever.** An app offers a dozen bells and lets you change your
mind. If Metty's tone grates after six months there is no setting — only a new bowl. This
is the honest cost of the thing that makes it good.

**5. Mains-only is a live objection in this specific category.** Dayasa markets battery
operation explicitly as avoiding electromagnetic radiation; the Zen Timepiece ran on C
cells. The buyer for a "wave-free" calm object may read a mains lead as a contradiction.
The engineering case for mains is strong and documented — but it needs pre-empting in the
pitch, not ignoring.

**6. Control legibility is where the incumbent actually died** — a 40-page manual for two
knobs. Four programme positions of which one is Off, two endless encoders whose knobs
cannot show their own values, and a hidden three-second hold gesture is *more* to explain,
not less. This is the risk the design most needs to keep testing.

**7. The Triratna framing is a knife that cuts both ways.** It makes the object
immediately legible and correct inside that tradition, and slightly alienating outside it.
It is a real differentiator and a real narrowing.

**8. No streaks, no logging, no community — deliberately.** The apps that win retention win
on exactly these. Defensible, and it also means the object has no mechanism to make you
use it tomorrow.

**9. Distribution is unsolved.** DharmaCrafts is sold out of everything; Plum Village sells
hand bells; Amazon is a race to $30. There is no obvious shelf — which is likely why Now &
Zen sold direct for 31 years and then stopped.

**10. Category viability is unproven.** Now & Zen died after 31 years. PrannaTimer is
gone. Punkt sells refurbs. This is a hobby-scale market where a few thousand units is a
*good* outcome, not a business.

**11. One person's build is not a product.** Everything above assumes a thing that could
be sold. As a one-off for its maker, the analysis is moot and the only question is whether
it is nicer to use than a phone — which it almost certainly is.

## What this implies for the build

Five things the research changes or confirms, in priority order:

1. **Fix the bowl properly.** The loose bowl was the single most-cited flaw in the product
   that owned this niche. The cradle work in `hardware/README.md` is not a detail; it is
   the competitor's cause of death.
2. **Guard control legibility above all.** Now & Zen lost on the manual, not the sound. If
   Metty needs explaining, it has the same disease. The hold-to-save gesture in particular
   is undiscoverable by design — worth deciding whether that is elegance or a defect.
3. **Do not price at £150–250.** That band is where the closest comparable product went to
   die. If this is ever sold, the evidence points to **£450–750**, justified by the bowl
   and the making rather than by the design language.
4. **Pre-empt the mains objection.** The reliability argument is good; the category expects
   batteries. Say why, early.
5. **Lead with the practice, not the bell.** The programme model is the only thing on the
   market that nothing else has. The bell is the reason to believe, not the claim.
